## Beat 3.1

Pull back to the whole continent and the truth appears: there is no American
grid. There are three. The Eastern Interconnection, the Western, and Texas —
each one machine within itself, each electrically deaf to the other two.
Alternating current cannot simply flow between them; their heartbeats don't
align.

## Beat 3.2

Texas is an island on purpose. In 1935, Washington claimed authority over any
utility whose power crossed a state line. The Texas utilities drew the obvious
conclusion: never cross the line. No interstate connection, no federal
oversight. One regulatory dodge, and ninety years later an entire state
balances alone.

## Beat 3.3

The three machines aren't perfectly sealed. A dozen-odd doors exist —
direct-current ties, where alternating current is converted to DC, slipped
across the border, and converted back. They are marked here. Their combined
capacity is a rounding error on any of the three machines: useful for trade,
nearly useless in a crisis. Remember that.

## Beat 3.4

There is a fourth machine. Québec — a province of dammed rivers — runs its own
interconnection and points it south. High-voltage DC lines carry the water's
work into New England and New York; on a cold winter morning, a real slice of
Boston's heat is falling water from the sub-Arctic. Canada isn't a footnote to
this grid. It's a shareholder.

## Beat 3.5

February 2021. A cold front parks over Texas, and the machine finally meets
its 1935 decision. Gas wells freeze. Plants trip offline — twenty thousand
megawatts of them — while demand sets winter records. Frequency sags below
59.4. ERCOT cuts power to four and a half million homes and businesses, in
the cold, for days, to stop a collapse that would have taken months to
repair. The island
could not borrow. There was nothing to borrow through. Two hundred and
forty-six people died.

## Beat 3.6

A blackout is not the grid running out, the way a car runs out of gas. It is
frequency collapse — the machine tearing itself apart — and the only defense
is amputation: cut whole neighborhoods loose, fast, to save the rest. When
balance returns, the lights come back the way they just did on this sheet.
The morning Texas nearly failed, the margin was minutes.

## Beat 3.7

Who decides what you pay? Two layers. Washington — a commission called FERC —
governs the wholesale layer: the auctions, the interstate wires. Your bill
belongs to fifty state commissions. And the country splits: in the tinted
states, you can choose your electricity supplier; in the rest, one utility
owns everything from plant to pole and a regulator sets its allowed profit.
Neither model has clearly won.

## Beat 3.8

Decode the bill. Roughly half of what you pay is the electricity itself — the
auctions from the day you just watched. The rest is delivery: the transmission
highways and the neighborhood wires, priced not by markets but by regulators,
because wires are natural monopolies. When your bill climbs these days, the
culprit is usually the wires, not the power.
