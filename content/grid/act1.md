## Beat 1.1

Start at the wall. Behind the outlet's two slots sits 120 volts, held there
day and night whether you use it or not. It has been waiting your whole life.
And nothing about it is stored — no tank, no reservoir, no battery worth
mentioning. Where does it come from, right now, this second? Follow the wire
backward.

## Beat 1.2

One rule runs the whole machine. Electricity is made in the same instant it is
used. Every second, the generation on the left of the scale must equal the
demand on the right, across an entire continent. Everything else in this story
— the auctions, the prices, the blackouts — is what enforcing that rule looks
like.

## Beat 1.3

The wire ends at a place like this. Lackawanna Energy Center, Jessup,
Pennsylvania: 1,500 megawatts of natural gas spinning shafts of copper and
steel. A generator is a simple thing at heart — a magnet turned inside a coil
of wire. Fire spins it; spinning makes current. Everything upstream of your
outlet exists to spin magnets.

## Beat 1.4

Electricity bleeds energy as heat when it travels. The trick that makes a
continental grid possible: push the voltage up, and the losses collapse. At
the plant's switchyard, transformers step the generator's output up to
345,000 volts — far too dangerous to touch, remarkably cheap to ship. Voltage
goes up to travel. It will come back down before it reaches you.

## Beat 1.5

Then it marches. Steel towers carry the line south and east, over ridgelines
and rivers, toward the load — the cities. This is transmission, the interstate
highway system of power: built for bulk, blind to individual addresses. The
faint lines on this sheet are the real ones, every circuit above 220,000
volts.

## Beat 1.6

At the city's edge, the highway exits. A substation — that fenced yard of gray
steel you drive past without seeing — steps the voltage down and splits the
power into feeders, neighborhood by neighborhood. From here on, the system
stops thinking in cities and starts thinking in streets.

## Beat 1.7

The last mile is wooden. Poles, a can-shaped transformer outside your window,
one more step down — 7,200 volts becomes the 120 at your wall. Most outages
are born here, not in some distant control room: a branch on a line, an ice
storm, a squirrel with bad luck. The machine's most delicate part is the part
you can see from your porch.

## Beat 1.8

That's the whole trip: fire to filament, a few hundred miles, at very nearly
the speed of light. You flipped a switch, and a generator in Pennsylvania
leaned into its work before your finger left the plastic. And along the entire
path — plant, wires, substation, pole — nothing stored anything.

## Beat 1.9

Now zoom out, because the wire you followed doesn't stop. East of the Rockies,
nearly every wire in America is connected to every other — one electrical
machine spanning half a continent, spinning in lockstep at 60 cycles a second.
The largest machine ever built, and it has no warehouse. Someone has to
balance it. Every second. Who?
