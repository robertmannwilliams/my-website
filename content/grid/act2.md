## Beat 2.1

Nobody owns the grid, but somebody has to conduct it. The balancing — supply
equals demand, every second — belongs to several dozen organizations called
balancing authorities. The biggest are markets as much as control rooms:
PJM in the mid-Atlantic, MISO down the middle, ERCOT in Texas, CAISO in
California. Their districts are drawn on this map. Watch one day cross them.

## Beat 2.2

Four in the morning in Pennsylvania. The Susquehanna plant hums in the dark —
2,500 megawatts, running flat out, the way it has most hours since 1983. A
nuclear plant works like this: cheap to run, slow to move, so you never move
it. Plants like this are called baseload. The country sleeps. The machine
doesn't.

## Beat 2.3

Six a.m., and the Eastern Seaboard wakes up one time zone at a time. A coffee
maker draws a thousand watts. Forty million of them, more or less at once, is
forty gigawatts — sixteen Susquehannas — and the morning has barely started.
Someone has to decide what turns on. There is an order to it.

## Beat 2.4

The order is an auction. Every five minutes, the market takes every plant's
offer — the price it needs to run — and switches them on cheapest-first until
supply meets demand. Engineers call the lineup the merit order. Drag the demand
line through a morning and watch it work.

## Beat 2.5

Here is the strange part. Every plant that runs gets paid the same price: the
offer of the last, most expensive plant needed. The cheap nuclear plant earns
whatever the final gas turbine asked for. It sounds broken. It's the incentive
doing the work — every plant fights to be cheap enough to clear, and the price
tells everyone exactly how tight the system is.

## Beat 2.6

Noon in California. The Solar Star fields north of Los Angeles pour out as
much power as a nuclear reactor, and they are a rounding error on what the
state's rooftops are doing. At midday the market can be so flooded that the
price touches zero — some hours it goes negative, and a plant pays to stay on.
Slide through the day and watch demand sag in the middle. Operators call the
shape the duck.

## Beat 2.7

Then the sun sets on all of it at once — right as everyone comes home and
turns everything on. The flood becomes a cliff. Covering it falls to peakers:
small, fast gas turbines built to run a few hundred hours a year, at whatever
price the evening will bear. This is the expensive hour. The lights you see
below are the machine spending money.

## Beat 2.8

While the ramp climbs, something else is happening underneath: the whole
Eastern half of the machine spins in lockstep at exactly 60 cycles a second,
and any gap between supply and demand drags the number off its mark. Let it
drift half a percent and hardware starts protecting itself. Hold the line
yourself — then know that a computer in the control room does this every four
seconds, forever.

## Beat 2.9

What changed in the last five years is parked in places like this. Menifee
Power Bank: 680 megawatts of batteries outside Los Angeles, charged at noon
when power is nearly free, sold back at seven when it isn't. On many California
evenings the battery fleet is briefly the biggest source on the state's grid.
The duck built the battery business, and the battery is eating the peaker's
dinner.

## Beat 2.10

Midnight in West Texas. At Horse Hollow the turbines run on night wind the
market barely pays for. Texas keeps some of the cheapest hours in America out
here — if you can be awake for them. Somewhere a control room is quietly
balancing all of it, and tomorrow at four a.m., Pennsylvania hums again. The
machine never sleeps. That's the whole point of it.
